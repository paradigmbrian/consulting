## Assessment of the evidence base

**Do not re-walk this. The domain has no publishable statistic for a trades win-back page.** The failure is structural, not a matter of searching harder.

**On the "5x acquisition cost" claim — definitively NOT sourceable, and worse than merely unsourced.** It is *affirmatively debunked* by a credible published source that a later task can cite if it ever needs to explain the rejection: Keiningham, Vavra, Aksoy & Wallard, *Loyalty Myths* (Wiley, 2005), excerpt published by Ipsos Loyalty at `https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/publication/2003-08/Ipsos_Loyalty_Myth_8_Excerpt.pdf` (fetched, HTTP 200, text extracted). The authors traced the claim to unpublished TARP work in the late 1980s and simultaneous identical claims by the Customer Service Institute, Consumer Connections Corp. and ITEM Group; it then entered "prestigious journals and books" (Hart/Heskett/Sasser's 1990 HBR "The Profitable Art of Service Recovery"; Tom Peters' *Thriving on Chaos*) purely by repetition. They give three structural reasons it is wrong, notably that advertising/promotion spend is misallocated to the acquisition side because much of it targets *existing* customers. **Never publish this figure.**

**On "5% retention → 25–95% profit" — not usable.** Two distinct sources get conflated. The *1990 HBR* article (Reichheld & Sasser, "Zero Defections: Quality Comes to Services") contains the industry-specific numbers (bank 85%, insurance 50%, **auto-service chain 30%**) and is the better population match — it is about *services*, and the auto-service chain is the closest analogue to a trades business anywhere in this literature. But it is **paywalled**: the URL returns 200 and the statistics are not on the free page. The famous **25–95%** range is not from that article at all — it is from the *1996 book* **The Loyalty Effect**. Bain's own page restates it as a third number ("almost 100%"). A book is not a study; a paywall is not a citable URL. All routes fail.

**The DOE Better Buildings Business Models Guide (2013) is the one genuinely primary, exactly-on-population source in this domain** — `https://www1.eere.energy.gov/buildings/betterbuildings/neighborhoods/pdfs/hvac_contractor_business_model.pdf` (HTTP 200; parse it locally with pypdf, WebFetch's extractor chokes on it). It yields no usable *statistic*, but it is excellent **body-copy support** for the page's premise, and it is quotable, attributable government material about HVAC contractors specifically:
- "The HVAC contractor's key revenue driver is **repeat business from maintenance contracts**."
- "**Direct interaction with customers through repeat service visits is the primary means of generating revenue** for HVAC contractors."
- "**Annual service and maintenance checks are the primary means by which HVAC contractors drive follow-on sales.**"
- "HVAC contractors indicated that they consider a portfolio of **500 service contracts** to be a reasonable threshold to ensure the sustainability of an HVAC business."
- Companion doc (`home_performance_contractor_business_model.pdf`): "most home performance contractor sales come from **repeat business or customer referrals**."

These are qualitative/structural claims. They cannot populate a `stats:` array without inventing a number — **do not manufacture a percentage from them.** They can honestly support prose like "the DOE's own guide to HVAC contractor business models says repeat business is the primary revenue driver."

**Why nothing transfers.** Retention/reactivation research is overwhelmingly e-commerce, retail, telecom, or newspaper subscription. The two structural axes both have to match and never do at once: *contractual vs non-contractual* (trades are non-contractual — customers lapse silently, they don't churn) and *industry purchase driver* (trades are need-driven and seasonal; a homeowner 18 months past an HVAC service is not deciding to re-engage a brand, they are waiting for something to break). The Holtrop & Wieringa paper matches on the first axis and is the best methodological analogue in existence, but its field test is greeting cards. Nothing matches both.

**One genuinely useful finding from Holtrop & Wieringa (2023), against interest and worth heeding in the copy:** naive reactivation blasts did **nothing**. "We found that the current firm business rule of sending mailings after two months of inactivity **did not increase activity**," and targeting customers *before* their expected purchase time can *increase* churn (extending Ascarza, Iyengar & Schleicher 2016). Timing relative to each customer's own interpurchase interval was the whole effect. The page should not imply that simply blasting a lapsed list works — the peer-reviewed evidence says it doesn't.

**Search hygiene for whoever picks this up.** Consistent with the sibling missed-call-text-back task, this space is saturated with unsourced vendor content: ServiceTitan, calljolt, ciwebgroup, hvac-seo.agency, cityranked, building36, fieldedge, zyratalk and contractor2020 all publish authoritative-looking retention/LTV benchmarks with no primary source. Treat any trades retention statistic as vendor marketing until proven otherwise. Two specific traps found here: (1) **the $40 retention figure does not exist in the DOE document it sits next to** — I searched both PDFs' full text; only the $200–300 acquisition half is real, so a spot-check of the link would wrongly appear to validate the whole sentence; (2) **`grokipedia.com` surfaces high in search results for both famous claims and is AI-generated** — not a source, do not cite. No wholly fabricated citation to a named real institution was confirmed in this workflow (unlike the sibling's fake Pew and BrightLocal studies), but the DOE case shows the softer failure mode: a real institution, a real live link, and a figure smuggled in beside it that the institution never published.
