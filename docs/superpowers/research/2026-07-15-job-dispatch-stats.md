## Accepted

### 1. Quoting a 4-hour arrival window instead of a 1-hour window drops first-impression customer satisfaction from 8.01 to 5.52 out of 10

- **value:** `8.01 → 5.52`
- **label:** Average customer satisfaction (1–10) when quoted a 1-hour vs. a 4-hour arrival window
- **source:** Zion & Zion, 2018
- **sourceUrl:** https://www.zionandzion.com/download/research/ConsumerSatisfactionAffectedbyPlumberWaitTimes.pdf
- **Verified:** URL fetched 2026-07-15 (HTTP 200) and text extracted in full. Claim present verbatim under the heading "NO ONE LIKES TO WAIT": *"respondents who were offered a one-hour service arrival window had an average satisfaction rating of 8.01. Respondents who were given a 4-hour service arrival window had an average satisfaction rating of 5.52."* Note the human-readable research page (`.../research/waiting-for-the-plumber-how-consumer-satisfaction-is-affected-by-wait-time/`) **301-redirects to this PDF** — cite the PDF directly. WebFetch cannot parse this PDF's binary; text was recovered by decoding its zlib streams (same technique the missed-call sibling used).
- **Population/conditions:** Nationwide survey of **1,044 US adult homeowners (age 25+)**. Authors: Aric Zion, MS; Thomas Hollmann, MBA, PhD, 2018. **Small operator, not enterprise fleet** — the population is residential plumbing *customers*, the correct population for a small trades business. **Not vendor-commissioned**: Zion & Zion sells no dispatch or scheduling software. **Conflict to state plainly:** Zion & Zion is a marketing agency, and this research is content marketing aimed at attracting home-services clients. That is a real but materially weaker conflict than a dispatch vendor publishing numbers that advertise its own product — the agency has no scheduling product to sell. Co-author Thomas Hollmann is a genuine academic (verified: Clinical Associate Professor of Marketing and Executive Director of the Center for Services Leadership, W. P. Carey School of Business, ASU; published in *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*) — though he was hired by Zion & Zion in 2017, so he is not an independent third party. **Also stated-preference, not observed behavior**: respondents rated "first-impression satisfaction in response to a promised wait window" — a hypothetical quote, not measured satisfaction after a real service call. **This study is NOT peer-reviewed**; it is an agency white paper. (Flagging explicitly because a web-search summarizer asserted during this research that it was "the primary peer-reviewed source on this topic" — that is false and a later task will likely see the same false claim.)
- **Why it applies here:** Directly on point — it is the customer-notification half of this workflow measured on exactly the right population. But **do not ship it alone**; see Outcome.

Two further figures from this same study were considered and deliberately **not** treated as separate stats:
- *"Each extra hour of window = 0.85 lower satisfaction (regression-controlled; 0.82 raw)"* — this is the **same finding** as the accepted figure, expressed as a slope instead of two endpoints. Publishing both would present one result as two independent data points. Dishonest. If a slope reads better on the page than the endpoints, swap it in — but it is one stat either way.
- *"City dwellers become less satisfied 24 minutes quicker than suburban/rural residents"* — real, distinct, and verified, but it measures a geographic moderator, not the dispatch value proposition. It would confuse a reader on a sales page rather than persuade. Not worth a slot.

## Assessment of the evidence base

Four things a later task should know:

1. **There is exactly one credible figure in this domain, and one is not enough.** The Zion & Zion 2018 plumber arrival-window study is real, fetchable, correctly populated (US homeowners, small-operator context), methodologically stated, and academically co-authored. It survives the gate. It is also **alone** — and its distinct numbers are all restatements of a single relationship (satisfaction falls ~0.85 per extra hour of quoted window). No amount of splitting turns one finding into a two-stat section. **Do not re-run this search hoping for a second: it was searched from the vendor angle, the academic angle, the government/BLS angle, and the independent-analyst angle. Everything else is vendor marketing.**

2. **The domain is exactly the trap the brief predicted, and then some.** Every page-one result for every dispatch-statistics query is a company selling dispatch software: Geotab, ServiceTitan, Workiz, FIELDBOSS, Repair-CRM, ServicePower, Housecall Pro, FieldEdge, Rev.io. The brief's nuance clause — vendors who commission genuinely independent research and publish full reports with methodology — **found no qualifying instance here**. TOA Technologies came closest (real survey, real outlets picked it up) and still fails: vendor-published, unstable figure, dead URL, wrong population. The nuance clause is a real door, but nothing in this domain walks through it.

3. **Confirmed likely fabrication: the "2025 Deloitte study — 12.3% labor cost reduction from balanced scheduling."** No such study surfaced on direct search; Deloitte's real scheduling material reports figures an order of magnitude smaller (0.5–2.5%). This extends the sibling's finding — the fabricated-citation pattern (a precise, quotable number attributed to a famous institution by a vendor blog) is **not confined to ainora.lt and hicira.com**; it now includes vendor CRM blogs citing Big Four consultancies. **The rule generalizes: never cite an institution because a vendor blog says it said something.** Separately, note that a web-search summarizer during this task asserted the Zion & Zion white paper was "the primary peer-reviewed source on this topic" — it is not peer-reviewed. **Search-result summaries in this domain invent provenance. Verify at the document.**

4. **BLS is real but has nothing to say here.** BLS publishes solid HVAC/plumber employment and wage data (425,200 HVAC jobs in 2024; plumber median wage $62,970 in May 2024). None of it measures dispatch efficiency, technician utilization, travel time, or scheduling — and the wage figures would be pricing-adjacent claims the site does not publish. The obvious "use government data" instinct is a dead end for this workflow.

**Recommendation for the implementation task:** ship `stats: []`. Argue the page from the mechanism — a small trades owner already knows what a blown appointment window and a badly routed van cost them. As the sibling concluded, borrowed enterprise-fleet numbers would make the page *less* credible to that reader, not more. If a stats section is ever wanted here, the two credible routes are (a) pair the Zion & Zion arrival-window finding with first-party measured results from PDS's own deployments, cited as such, or (b) ship the Zion figure alone in prose — as a cited sentence in the body copy, not as a `stats` tile — where a single honest data point does not masquerade as a statistical case.
