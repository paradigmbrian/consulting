## Assessment of the evidence base

**Bottom line: there is no primary, correctly-populated statistic about small trades businesses losing time to duplicate data entry between tools. It does not exist. Do not re-research this.**

The two claim families the brief flagged both resolve cleanly, and later tasks *will* encounter them:

1. **"The average company uses N apps" — resolved, reject.** This traces to **Okta's *Businesses at Work*** (101 in 2025, 93 in 2024) and its SaaS-management peers (Productiv, BetterCloud). Okta's report is *real* — dated, methodologically stated, and live (I fetched it and confirmed the claim). It fails anyway, and the reason is worth stating precisely because its surface quality is so high: Okta's own methodology text says it measures **"enterprise software decisions"** across **"customers in the Okta Integration Network"** — i.e., apps deployed via OIN by organizations that buy enterprise SSO — with **no company-size breakdown and no SMB segment**. It counts a denominator that a three-van plumbing company is not in and cannot be in. It is not a fact about small businesses and cannot be relabeled into one. **The figure is sound; the population is disqualifying.**

2. **"Workers spend X% of their time on manual data entry / app-switching" — resolved, reject.** Three canonical members, all dead: **Smartsheet 2017** (40%+ of workers, quarter of the week) is a work-management vendor's own report. **HBR/Soroco 2022** (1,200 toggles/day, ~4 hrs/week) is vendor research wearing an HBR byline — I confirmed the Soroco/Workfabric affiliations on the page — with a **137-user, three-Fortune-500 sample**, and the article is **paywalled**, so the numbers are not even verifiable at the URL people cite. **McKinsey MGI 2012** (~20% of the week / the derived "1.8 hours/day") is consultancy thought-leadership arguing for the technology it recommends, and — decisively — **mckinsey.com would not load from this environment across two attempts**, so there is no fetchable primary to cite even if one wanted to. The brief predicted this one would be "hard to trace to a fetchable primary"; that is exactly what happened.

Three further findings worth carrying forward:

3. **The conflict of interest here is structural, not incidental.** In the missed-call domain, vendors *dominated* the evidence base. Here it is stronger: **only vendors have the data.** Measuring how much time someone wastes retyping data between apps requires instrumenting their apps — which only iPaaS, work-graph, and SaaS-management companies do, and they do it on the customers who bought their software. The measurement apparatus and the sales motive are the same apparatus. There is no neutral observer in this space and there is no mechanism by which one could appear. This is why no amount of additional searching will help.

4. **The best government instrument does not ask the question — I checked.** Census **BTOS** is the ideal candidate on paper: genuinely primary, ~1.2M employer businesses, **includes very small firms**, biweekly releases, and it already carries a technology supplement. I fetched its FAQ (2026-07-15): its subject areas are **business performance, operating status, employment, revenue, prices, demand, extreme-weather impact, and AI adoption** (AI use ran 17–20% Dec 2025–May 2026, across 15 business functions). **Nothing on software integration, duplicate data entry, admin time, or app counts.** Census **ABS** likewise covers R&D, innovation, and technology *types* used — not time lost to re-keying. The reason this statistic doesn't exist isn't that I failed to find it; it's that the only survey with the right population has never asked.

5. **The population trap here is worse than in the missed-call domain, and inverts the pitch.** Every surviving figure describes a salaried knowledge worker toggling between Salesforce, Slack, and Jira inside a Fortune 500. The buyer is an owner-operator who quotes a job on a phone at a jobsite and re-keys the customer into QuickBooks that night. Publishing "the average company uses 101 apps" or "workers toggle 1,200 times a day" on a page aimed at that reader doesn't just risk being wrong — it **actively signals the page wasn't written for them**, which is the precise credibility failure a stats section exists to prevent.

**Recommendation:** ship `stats: []`. The pain is self-evident to the buyer — they are the one doing the retyping, and they know exactly how long it takes because they do it after hours. Argue from the mechanism ("you enter the customer once"), not from borrowed enterprise numbers. If a stats section is ever wanted here, the only defensible source is **first-party measured data from PDS's own deployments, cited as such** — e.g. time-on-task measured before and after for a named client, with permission.
