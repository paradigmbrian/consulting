# New-Customer Onboarding — Stats Research

**Date:** 2026-07-15
**For:** `/services/automated-workflows/new-customer-onboarding`

## Outcome

**No figures survived the acceptance gate. This workflow ships `stats: []`.** Zero accepted, nine rejected.

The plan classified this workflow as "uncertain — research honestly and accept the result." The honest result is that **the research does not exist for this population.** The trap the brief predicted is exactly what the evidence base turned out to be: "customer onboarding" as a research topic is owned by SaaS, and every usable-looking number is either (a) published by a company selling onboarding software, or (b) measured on SaaS trial users. Neither describes a homeowner who booked a pool cleaning and needs a welcome, an agreement, and a first visit on the calendar.

There *is* genuine, high-quality primary research adjacent to this workflow — the Cochrane review of SMS appointment reminders. It is rejected, on two independent grounds, and it is the candidate most likely to be rediscovered and re-litigated later. See the rejection table and the assessment.

## Assessment of the evidence base

**Do not re-research this domain. Four angles were searched to exhaustion and all four are closed:**

1. **"Customer onboarding" as a search term is owned by SaaS, and the top of the funnel is 100% vendor.** The brief predicted this and it is precisely correct. Searching onboarding + retention returns UserGuiding, GUIDEcx, Paddle, SundaySky, Docebo, ProfitWell, Userpilot, Appcues, Gainsight — every result is a company selling onboarding software, and every population is trial users, app installs, and subscription churn. There is no consumer-services onboarding literature underneath it. The aggregator `shno.co` was checked directly: its entire source list is SaaS vendors. **The dominant failure here is population, and it is unfixable by better searching** — nobody has studied what a welcome email and a signed agreement do to a pool-cleaning customer's retention, because there is no product category funding that research.

2. **The genuinely good research in this space is about the wrong thing.** Appointment-reminder research is real, primary, free, and high quality (Cochrane 2013; AJM 2010). It is also (a) about clinic patients and (b) about *reminding* someone of a booked appointment. **This workflow books the visit; it does not remind.** A later task will find the Cochrane review, notice it passes criteria 1–3 cleanly, and want to use it. It was read in full and rejected on criterion 4 — twice over. Note also that the AJM RCT found the *human* reminder outperformed the *automated* one, so the highest-quality evidence nearest this workflow mildly contradicts the automation pitch. Reinstating it would be worse than shipping nothing.

3. **A note on fabrication — different from the sibling's finding, and worth recording accurately.** The sibling missed-call task found aggregators inventing citations to real institutions (the non-existent "2024 Pew voicemail survey"; the non-existent "BrightLocal Local Consumer Communication Study"). **In this domain I found no fabricated institutional citations.** The aggregators here (`shno.co`, `electroiq.com`) fail differently: they cite *real* vendor sources accurately, and the vendor sources are simply worthless as evidence. The one attribution that does not hold up is the **"Forrester $1 → $5" onboarding claim** — Forrester's real dollar-return research is about UX ease-of-use ($10–$100), not onboarding, and no Forrester onboarding publication with this figure could be located. Treat it as misattributed rather than confirmed-fabricated; either way it is uncitable (paywalled + unlocatable). This distinction matters: not every aggregator is a fabricator, and saying so falsely would be its own error.

4. **Cross-check against the sibling results.** Three workflows are now researched: missed-call-text-back accepted 0 of 17, review-generation accepted 3 of 10 (BrightLocal's *Local Consumer Review Survey* is a real, methodologically disclosed primary survey), and this one accepts 0 of 9. The pattern is legible and useful: **a workflow has publishable stats only when a genuine independent survey happens to study consumers of local services** (review-reading behaviour). Where the topic is an *internal operational process* — missed calls, onboarding — the only parties who measure it are the vendors selling the fix, and there is nothing to cite. Expect the same outcome for job-dispatch, tool-sync, and weekly-owner-reports; expect invoice-reminders and automated-quoting to be similarly vendor-saturated.

**Recommendation:** ship `stats: []` and let the page argue from the mechanism. The reader is a trades owner who already knows what it costs them to hand-write a welcome, chase a signed agreement, and find a first slot — the spec's own persona puts it at ~20 minutes per customer. That is a more persuasive argument to that reader than a SaaS activation statistic would be, and a borrowed number they recognise as not-about-them would actively cost credibility. If a stats section is wanted later, the only honest source is **first-party data from PDS's own deployments, cited as such.**
