# Review Generation — Stats Research

**Date:** 2026-07-15
**For:** `/services/automated-workflows/review-generation`

## Outcome

**Three figures accepted, seven rejected.** All three accepted figures come from a single source — BrightLocal's *Local Consumer Review Survey 2026* — which, unlike the missed-call-text-back space, is a real, dated, methodologically disclosed primary survey that I fetched and read in situ.

Resulting `stats:` value: three entries (97% read reviews / 74% want reviews from the last three months / 68% only use 4+ star businesses). If only two are wanted, **drop the 97%** — see the note on it below.

Unlike the sibling workflow, this domain *does* have a genuine primary source underneath it. The spec's expectation was correct here.

## Accepted

### 1. Nearly all consumers read online reviews for local businesses
- **value:** 97%
- **label:** US consumers who say they read online reviews for local businesses
- **source:** BrightLocal, 2026
- **sourceUrl:** https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/
- **Verified:** URL fetched 2026-07-15, claim present in the bullet-point key-findings summary at the top of the page and again under the heading "Consumers Read Reviews More Than Ever, But How They Discover Them is Changing". Stated as "97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses"; the body prose renders it as "Reviews are a vital part of the business decision-making process, with 97% of consumers reading reviews online."
- **Population/conditions:** Representative panel of 1,002 US adult consumers surveyed via SurveyMonkey; age distribution 18–29 (22%), 30–44 (25%), 45–59 (28%), 60+ (25%). Published 11 Feb 2026. Self-reported behaviour, not observed. The survey instrument is about local businesses throughout, but BrightLocal does **not** publish the question wording or which response options were counted toward the 97% (i.e. whether "rarely"/"sometimes" are included — almost certainly yes, given 41% say they "always" read reviews).
- **Why it applies here:** **Weak — consider dropping.** It is true to source and it is the survey's own headline, but it is the single most-recycled statistic in local marketing and a skeptical trades owner has seen it on every vendor's page. Its scope on the page is also slightly loose (headline says "for local businesses", prose says "online"). It establishes baseline relevance and nothing more. The other two do real work; this one does not.

### 2. Consumers discount reviews older than a few months
- **value:** 74%
- **label:** US consumers who look for reviews written within the last three months
- **source:** BrightLocal, 2026
- **sourceUrl:** https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/
- **Verified:** URL fetched 2026-07-15, claim present under the heading "Does review recency really matter in consumer decision-making? Emphatically, yes." Stated as "74% seek reviews written in the last three months." Adjacent figures in the same section: 32% look for reviews written in the last two weeks (up from 20%), and 18% are only swayed by reviews written in the last week.
- **Population/conditions:** Same survey as above — 1,002 US adult consumers, SurveyMonkey panel, published 11 Feb 2026. Self-reported.
- **Why it applies here:** **Strongest of the three, and the one the page should lead with.** This is the exact mechanism the product addresses: a review isn't a one-time asset, it decays. A business with forty five-year-old reviews and nothing recent looks stale to three-quarters of the people checking it. That is precisely the argument for automating a steady flow of requests rather than doing a one-off review push. The survey's own 2026 headline is "Star Ratings Keep Rising, Old Reviews Don't Cut It."

### 3. Most consumers filter out businesses below four stars
- **value:** 68%
- **label:** US consumers who say they will only use a business rated four stars or higher
- **source:** BrightLocal, 2026
- **sourceUrl:** https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/
- **Verified:** URL fetched 2026-07-15, claim present under the heading "Star Ratings Keep Rising with Consumers Demanding Four or More". Stated as "Seven in ten (68%) will only use a business with four or more stars, up from 55% in 2025."
- **Population/conditions:** Same survey — 1,002 US adult consumers, SurveyMonkey panel, published 11 Feb 2026. Self-reported stated preference, which typically overstates real-world strictness.
- **Why it applies here:** Supports the "ask your *happy* customers, systematically" framing — the rating threshold is a filter a trades business either clears or doesn't. **Alternative available from the same section if a punchier number is wanted:** "In 2026, 31% of consumers will only use a business with 4.5 stars or more" (up from 17% in 2025), verified in situ alongside the 68%. I picked 68% as the more robust of the two; 31% is a bigger year-over-year jump but a smaller, more brittle base.

## Assessment of the evidence base

**The headline difference from the sibling workflow: this domain has a real primary source, and it is BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey.** It has run annually since at least 2015 (editions verified on BrightLocal's index back to 2015), publishes its sample size, panel provider, fielding period, and age distribution, and the 2026 edition is current as of five months ago. The brief's steer was right, and the discipline it prescribed — go to brightlocal.com's own research index, find the report, read the number in situ — is exactly what worked. Every figure above was read on BrightLocal's own page, never taken from someone quoting BrightLocal.

**Four things a later task should know:**

1. **The BrightLocal conflict of interest is real and should be disclosed internally, but does not fail criterion 1.** BrightLocal sells local SEO and reputation-management tooling, and "everyone reads reviews, they go stale fast, and you need 4+ stars" is a direct advertisement for that tooling. That is a genuine incentive problem and the figures should be read as the high end of plausible. But criterion 1's target is "an agency blog citing an agency blog," and this is the opposite: BrightLocal is the *publisher of its own primary survey*, with disclosed methodology and a 15-year series that can be checked for year-over-year consistency. That is categorically different from Podium or Birdeye asserting a number with no methodology. It passes. It is not above suspicion.

2. **The known methodological limits, stated plainly:** n=1,002 is small for the sub-group splits; it is a SurveyMonkey opt-in panel, not a probability sample; every figure is *self-reported stated preference*, which reliably overstates how strict people actually are in the moment; and BrightLocal does not publish question wording or response-option groupings, so the exact construction of the 97% in particular can't be audited. Some 2026 year-over-year jumps are also large enough to raise an eyebrow (68% from 55%; 31% from 17%; 41% "always" from 29%) — either consumer standards genuinely moved fast, or the instrument changed. BrightLocal doesn't say which. None of this is disqualifying for a marketing page that names the source and lets the reader click through, which is the whole point of the gate. It does mean these should never be presented as hard measured facts — they are what a thousand people told a survey.

3. **There is no trades-specific review research. Stop looking.** BrightLocal publishes no industry breakdown, and the searches for HVAC/plumbing/contractor review statistics return agency blogs exclusively. The honest framing is that these are *local business* consumer figures, applied to a local business. The `label` fields above say "local business," never "trades" or "home services" — keep it that way.

4. **The laundering pattern to watch for is population swap, not fabrication.** Unlike the missed-call space (where numbers were invented outright), the review space mostly takes *real* research and silently moves it to a new population: Luca's Seattle restaurants become "contractors," BrightLocal's "local businesses" become "local service providers," Pew's e-commerce shoppers become "consumers choosing a plumber." The number stays roughly right, so it survives a casual sniff test — which makes it more dangerous, not less. Always check what population the original actually measured.

**Recommendation:** ship the stats section with the 74% recency figure leading, 68% second, and 97% third or dropped. All three cite "BrightLocal, 2026" pointing at https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/ — a page a skeptical reader can open and find the number on. Do not add a fourth from a vendor blog to round it out.
