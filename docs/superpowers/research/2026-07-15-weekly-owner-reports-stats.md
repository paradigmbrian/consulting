# Weekly Owner Reports — Stats Research

**Date:** 2026-07-15
**For:** `/services/automated-workflows/weekly-owner-reports`

## Outcome

**Zero accepted, six rejected. This workflow ships `stats: []`.**

This is the outcome the plan anticipated, and the search confirms the reasoning behind it. The specific behavior this page sells — *a small-business owner receives a recurring summary of their operating numbers* — has not been studied. Not by government, not by academia, not by anyone independent. What exists is two clusters, and neither can be honestly relabeled:

1. **Real primary research about small businesses** (Federal Reserve SBCS, JPMorgan Chase Institute) that measures credit access, cash flow, and revenue — and says **nothing** about reporting, tracking, or reviewing numbers.
2. **Real research about reporting and data-driven decisions** (Brynjolfsson et al.) whose population is **large publicly traded firms and manufacturing plants** — not a plumber reading an email on Monday.

The overlap of those two circles is empty. See "Assessment" below — it is specific, so nobody re-runs this.

## Assessment of the evidence base

The plan predicted nothing defensible exists here. That prediction is correct, and the reason is structural rather than a gap that more searching would close:

1. **The subject has no literature because it is not a research question.** Nobody funds a study on "does emailing an owner their weekly numbers help." Academia studies *management accounting in SMEs* — and that literature openly describes itself as thin ("management accounting in SMEs remains largely under-researched," a point repeatedly made across the field's own review papers). What little exists studies whether small firms keep books at all, mostly via small self-reported surveys in developing economies. Government (SBA Advocacy, Fed SBCS, BLS) measures firm demographics, credit, survival, and employment — **not internal management practices.** No agency asks how often an owner looks at their numbers. There is no dataset to mine.

2. **The two adjacent literatures fail in opposite, non-fixable directions.** Research with the *right population* (JPMorgan Chase Institute's 597,000 small businesses; the Fed's SBCS) does not measure reporting behavior at all. Research measuring the *right behavior* (Brynjolfsson's DDD work) is about large publicly traded firms and manufacturing plants — where "data-driven decision-making" means analysts and data warehouses, not an email. **Bridging that gap requires an inference the sources do not make.** Any figure claiming to bridge it is fabricated or laundered.

3. **The specific trap here is a statistic that is *about weekly financial review* and traces to a 404.** The "95% weekly / 25–35% yearly" figure is dangerous in a way the missed-call research's traps were not: it is not adjacent, it is *on the nose*, and it carries an sba.gov link that survives casual inspection. **It is a dead link to a consultant's slide deck, mis-attributed to a misnamed agency, asserting a 95% small-business success rate that contradicts BLS survival data.** Anyone re-researching this workflow will find it within two searches. It is documented at length above so that it dies here.

4. **Consistent with the missed-call findings.** No fabricated *institutional* citation was confirmed in this search (nothing on the scale of the invented Pew/BrightLocal studies), though the SBA link above is functionally the same failure — a real institution's name and URL attached to a claim the institution never published. The AI-aggregator sites recur in this vertical too (`worldmetrics.org`, and assorted "N small business statistics 2026" listicles surfaced repeatedly); treat them as unusable. Vendor dominance is as bad as predicted: bookkeeping firms, CPAs, Quicken, Intuit, and BI vendors publish exactly the statistics that sell reporting — an automatic criterion-1 failure.

5. **The honest position is that this workflow argues from mechanism, not evidence.** An owner who doesn't know last week's revenue knows that's a problem without a citation. A stats section built from Ghanaian bookkeeping surveys or Fortune-500 productivity econometrics would *reduce* credibility with a trades reader, because the numbers would visibly not be about them. **`stats: []` is the stronger page.**

**Recommendation:** ship `stats: []`. If a stats section is ever wanted here, the only credible source is first-party data from PDS's own deployments, cited as such — the same conclusion the missed-call research reached, for the same reason.
